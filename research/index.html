ABDC A* Information & Management

Why augmented reality sells: a means-end framework

In One Line AR works in marketing because its features deliver concrete benefits that serve consumers' deeper personal values.

Marketers know augmented reality (AR) often outperforms other formats, but nobody had explained why from the consumer's side. Using means-end chain theory, we traced specific AR features such as contextualization, interactivity and portability to benefits such as inspiration, better decisions, time savings and lower risk. Those benefits in turn serve personal goals such as self-confidence, self-expression and less purchase regret.

ABDC A Marketing Intelligence & Planning

Meta-analysis of augmented reality marketing

In One Line Interactivity and augmentation are the two AR attributes that matter most for consumers.

Studies disagreed on whether AR attributes create fun (hedonic) value or practical (utilitarian) value. We pooled the statistical results of 19 quantitative AR marketing studies to settle which attributes create which value, and how that value shapes consumers' intention to buy. It was one of the first meta-analyses in AR marketing.

ABDC A Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services

AR immersion and privacy concerns

In One Line AR helps shoppers learn about products, but privacy worries weaken that learning.

AR can make virtual products feel physically present in your room, a sense called spatial presence. Past results on its effects were mixed, and nobody had tested how privacy worries affect what consumers learn from AR. We examined how spatial presence and cognitive involvement shape consumers' confidence in, and attitudes toward, products.

ABDC A Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services

ARvolution: why consumers use AR

In One Line People find AR useful for five distinct kinds of value, not just convenience.

Most AR adoption research uses the technology acceptance model, which asks whether people find a tool useful but not what value they get from it. We combined it with consumption value theory, testing five value dimensions (convenience, informational, emotional/hedonic, monetary and social) as the sources of AR's perceived usefulness.

ABDC A Journal of Business Research

Inclusive marketing: review and research agenda

In One Line A refined definition of inclusive marketing and a map of where the field stands.

Diverse, multicultural markets have made inclusive marketing a fast-growing topic, but its definition and how consumers respond to it were unclear. We reviewed 71 articles using the TCCM framework (theories, contexts, characteristics, methods). The review sorts the field into six streams, from representation of diverse consumers to critical views of inclusive marketing.

ABDC A Journal of Services Marketing

Services for consumers with disabilities

In One Line A framework for making services accessible at every stage, from planning to after-use.

Research on serving consumers with disabilities was scattered across disciplines. We reviewed 77 articles following the PRISMA protocol and organised findings by service design stage: planning, usage and post-usage. We then proposed a framework and a research agenda based on the Double Diamond design model.

Consumers interact with augmented reality differently across touchpoints—from browsing casually at home to checking dimensions inside a physical retail store.
